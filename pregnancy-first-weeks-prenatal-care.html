Pregnancy & Prenatal Care

Pregnant or Think You Might Be? What to Do in the First Weeks

A positive test creates a long list of questions. The safest first step is not to solve pregnancy alone—it is to establish prenatal care early, review medications and supplements, and know which symptoms should not wait.

Prepared for Canby Community Clinic•Updated August 14, 2026•10 min read•Editorial Standard
Medical information noticeThis article is general education. It does not diagnose a condition or replace prenatal or primary care from a qualified clinician. If you may be experiencing a medical emergency, call 911 or local emergency services.
Editorial methodThis article uses current guidance from major public-health and professional medical organizations. It separates general education from individual medical advice and does not claim a named medical reviewer who has not reviewed the final page.

The first weeks of pregnancy can feel medically quiet even while a great deal is changing. Some people have nausea, breast tenderness, fatigue, or frequent urination. Others have few symptoms. Symptoms alone cannot tell you whether a pregnancy is healthy, how far along it is, or what care you will need. The useful move is to shift from guessing to a plan.

Current guidance from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) emphasizes an early, comprehensive assessment and then a prenatal schedule tailored to the patient rather than one identical calendar for everyone. That means two people with the same estimated due date may appropriately have different visit schedules.

Start prenatal care early

ACOG's 2025 guidance recommends a comprehensive prenatal needs assessment early in pregnancy—ideally before 10 weeks of gestation or when the patient first presents for care. That first assessment is more than confirming pregnancy. It helps establish dating, medical and pregnancy history, medications, mental-health and social needs, and which laboratory tests or referrals are appropriate.

If you have a positive home pregnancy test, call an obstetric, family-medicine, or other prenatal-care clinician and ask when they want to see you. If you are unsure where to begin, a primary-care clinic can help you understand referral options, but do not assume every primary-care site provides obstetric care. Call Canby Community Clinic to ask which pregnancy-related services or referrals are currently available.

How many prenatal visits?

There is no longer one medically correct number for every pregnancy. ACOG recommends tailoring the schedule to medical and social needs. Evidence reviewed in its 2025 consensus supports roughly six to ten targeted prenatal visits for many average-risk patients, while patients with complications or additional needs may require more frequent in-person care, testing, or specialist visits. Your clinician should give you the schedule that applies to you.

Know what the first visit is trying to establish

The first prenatal evaluation typically builds a baseline. Your care team may ask about the first day of your last menstrual period, prior pregnancies, chronic conditions, surgeries, allergies, medications and supplements, family history, tobacco or substance exposure, mental health, housing or food concerns, and safety at home. Some of these questions can feel personal because they affect pregnancy risk and the support you may need.

Testing varies by timing and history. The point is not to order every possible test. It is to identify the information that changes care: pregnancy dating, blood type and Rh status, anemia or infection risk, immunity, and other conditions based on personal history and guideline recommendations.

Take folic acid—and bring every supplement to the conversation

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends 400 micrograms of folic acid each day for people who can become pregnant because folic acid helps prevent major neural-tube defects. Prenatal vitamins commonly contain folic acid, but the label matters. Some people need a different dose because of specific medical or pregnancy history, so higher doses should be discussed with a clinician rather than assumed to be better.

Bring a list or photos of every vitamin, herbal product, over-the-counter medication, and prescription you use. “Natural” does not automatically mean safe in pregnancy, and abruptly stopping an important prescription can also be harmful. Ask the prescribing clinician or prenatal-care clinician before starting or stopping medication whenever possible.

Avoid alcohol during pregnancy

CDC guidance is direct: there is no known safe amount of alcohol during pregnancy and no known safe time to drink during pregnancy. Wine, beer, and liquor all contain alcohol. If you drank before realizing you were pregnant, stop now and discuss the exposure with your prenatal-care clinician without hiding it. The purpose is to guide care, not to punish disclosure.

What common early symptoms can—and cannot—tell you

Fatigue, nausea, vomiting, breast tenderness, food aversions, mild cramping, and urinary frequency can occur in early pregnancy. Their presence or absence does not reliably establish pregnancy health. A person can have strong symptoms and still need evaluation; another can have few symptoms and have a normal pregnancy.

Because symptoms overlap with other conditions, use a pregnancy test and clinical evaluation rather than symptom-checking alone when pregnancy is possible. If a home test is negative but your period remains late or symptoms continue, ask a clinician whether and when to repeat testing.

Bleeding and pain deserve a clear threshold

Bleeding can occur in pregnancy and does not always mean pregnancy loss, but it should be discussed with a prenatal-care clinician. Pain and bleeding can also be signs of ectopic pregnancy, which can become life-threatening if it ruptures.

Seek emergency care now for severe warning signs

Sudden or severe abdominal or pelvic pain, shoulder pain, marked weakness, dizziness, fainting, heavy bleeding, trouble breathing, chest pain, seizure, or another rapidly worsening symptom should not wait for a routine appointment or website message. Call 911 or go to an emergency department as appropriate.

Build a medication and history list before the appointment

A short preparation list makes prenatal care more useful. Write down your prescription medications with doses, over-the-counter products, vitamins and supplements, allergies, prior surgeries, prior pregnancy outcomes, major family conditions, and the approximate date of your last menstrual period. Include the names of clinicians who manage chronic conditions.

If you have diabetes, high blood pressure, seizure disorder, kidney disease, thyroid disease, a history of blood clots, significant mental-health conditions, or another ongoing medical problem, tell the prenatal team early. Pregnancy may change monitoring or medication decisions, but those decisions should be individualized.

Questions worth asking at the first prenatal visit

  • How are you estimating the due date, and do I need an ultrasound for dating or another reason?
  • Which medications and supplements should I continue, stop, or change?
  • Which prenatal vitamin ingredients and doses do you recommend for me?
  • What symptoms should make me call the office, go to urgent care, or go to the emergency department?
  • What is my planned visit schedule, and what would make that schedule change?
  • Which labs, vaccines, genetic screening options, or referrals should we discuss?
  • Who do I contact after hours if something changes?

Prenatal care is a sequence, not one appointment

Good prenatal care changes as pregnancy changes. Early visits establish history and baseline testing. Later care monitors maternal health, fetal growth and development, vaccination needs, symptoms, and preparation for birth and postpartum care. A tailored schedule can include in-person and telehealth encounters when clinically appropriate, but required examinations, measurements, laboratory testing, imaging, or procedures still need the right setting.

The most useful outcome of each encounter is a clear next step: what happens next, when it happens, what results you are waiting for, and which symptoms should change the plan.
